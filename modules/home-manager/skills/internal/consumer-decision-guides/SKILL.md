# Writing Consumer Decision Guides

## Purpose

Help readers choose what to buy or use through practical, evidence-led advice. Treat “Wirecutter-inspired” as a request for consumer-journalism principles—clear recommendations, transparent reasoning, useful alternatives, and honest limits—not imitation of a publication’s distinctive voice, phrasing, branding, or formatting. Write in an original voice.

Use this for consumer products and services. Adapt the depth and structure to the decision; a simple comparison does not need to become an exhaustive buying guide.

## Workflow

1. **Define the decision.** Identify the reader, use case, budget, location, must-haves, and constraints. Ask a focused question if a missing detail could change the recommendation. Otherwise state reasonable assumptions.
2. **Set the criteria.** Choose a short list of factors that matter for this reader and explain why. Consider purchase price and ongoing costs, performance, usability, durability, support, accessibility, privacy, and environmental impact when relevant. Do not invent numeric weights or scores.
3. **Research and compare.** Prefer primary sources for specifications, policies, warranty terms, availability, and prices. Cross-check important performance or reliability claims with independent testing, credible reviews, expert sources, or other suitable evidence. Note conflicting or thin evidence instead of smoothing it over.
4. **Make a reader-specific recommendation.** Select a best fit only when the evidence supports it. Explain who benefits, why it wins on the chosen criteria, what it gives up, and when another option is better. “Best for most people” is a claim that needs broad evidence; do not use it as a default label.
5. **Draft and verify.** Put the useful answer first, make comparisons easy to scan, and audit every factual claim, price, date, and description of testing before finalizing.

## Evidence and transparency

- Use browsing or supplied source material when available. Link or cite sources near the claims they support, and give prices, availability, and other fast-changing facts a date or clear time context.
- Distinguish **hands-on testing**, **independent research**, **expert opinion**, and **manufacturer claims**. Say what was actually done, by whom, and under what conditions when that information is known.
- Never claim to have bought, used, tested, measured, interviewed, or verified something unless that work really happened. Never invent test results, quotes, expert endorsements, review consensus, citations, current prices, or product availability.
- If research tools or sources are unavailable, say so. Offer a clearly labeled preliminary framework or draft based on supplied information, identify the facts that still need verification, and avoid presenting an unverified winner as established fact.
- Separate evidence from judgment: tell readers which findings are observed facts and which are editorial interpretation. Report meaningful uncertainty and source disagreements.
- Disclose known sponsorship, affiliate relationships, or other relevant commercial interests. Do not let commission, access, or a supplied shortlist determine the editorial ranking; evaluate products against the reader’s needs.

## Article structure

Use only sections that help the reader decide. A full guide often benefits from:

1. **Headline and short answer:** Name the decision and give the lead recommendation with its best-fit reader and main reason.
2. **Other good choices:** Offer a small number of meaningful alternatives (for example, lower-cost, premium, or specialized), with the specific tradeoff that makes each worthwhile.
3. **Comparison table:** Compare decision-relevant facts consistently. Mark unknown or unverified entries rather than filling gaps with guesses.
4. **Why this is the pick:** Connect evidence to the criteria and explain important limitations, not just features.
5. **How we researched or tested:** Describe the actual process and its boundaries. Do not imply hands-on testing when the work was desk research.
6. **What to consider before buying:** Cover compatibility, ongoing costs, sizing, subscription renewal, cancellation, warranty, service area, or other category-specific friction where relevant.
7. **Sources and update context:** Make it possible to check consequential claims and understand when volatile information was verified.

For a shorter comparison, keep the short answer, decisive evidence, tradeoffs, and caveats; omit the rest.

## Editorial style

- Be direct, calm, and useful. Prefer concrete reasons over superlatives and promotional language.
- Write for a person making a decision, not for search keywords or a product catalog. Explain unfamiliar terms and put the most consequential information early.
- Make tradeoffs explicit. A product can be a good choice without being right for everyone; say who should skip it and why.
- Avoid false precision, unsupported consensus, feature dumps, filler introductions, and rankings that imply more certainty than the evidence allows.
- Do not reproduce recognizable Wirecutter/NYT language or present the article as affiliated with or endorsed by them.

## Example recommendation

> **Illustrative only:** Model A is the better fit for readers who need a compact option and prioritize battery life: its published dimensions meet that need, and current independent testing supports the battery comparison. It costs more than Model B, and we did not assess long-term reliability. Choose Model B if lower upfront cost matters more.

The example makes the audience, evidence basis, tradeoff, and limit visible without claiming a universal winner.

## Final check

- [ ] Does the recommendation answer a specific reader need?
- [ ] Can each important factual claim be traced to evidence?
- [ ] Are testing and research described accurately?
- [ ] Are current prices, policies, and availability dated or qualified?
- [ ] Are meaningful alternatives and drawbacks included?
- [ ] Are unknowns labeled, not guessed?
- [ ] Is the voice original, clear, and free of promotional or imitation language?

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Calling a product “tested” after reading its specifications | Describe the work as desk research and name the sources used |
| Naming a winner before defining the reader or criteria | Establish the use case and decision factors first |
| Treating the lowest listed price as the lowest cost | Include relevant recurring fees, accessories, shipping, and renewal terms |
| Turning uncertain claims into a confident ranking | Narrow the claim, explain uncertainty, or withhold the pick pending verification |
| Giving every candidate equal space | Prioritize the few choices that solve distinct reader needs |
